Slide 1 — Automated Cervical Spine Morphometry
- Today we are presenting our automated cervical spine MRI analysis pipeline.
- The core idea is simple: we start from a sagittal cervical MRI, extract the important anatomical structures, convert them into clinically meaningful measurements, assess those measurements, and finally generate a report-ready output.
- We designed this as a clinical support tool for radiologists, not as a replacement for radiologists.
- Our team focused on building something that is technically structured, clinically relevant, and honest about its limitations.

Slide 2 — Table of Contents
- I’ll begin with the clinical problem we are trying to address.
- Then I’ll show the overall solution and how the pipeline is organized technically.
- After that, I’ll walk through the architecture stage by stage: segmentation, measurements, assessement, and reporting.
- I’ll then discuss validation, including what worked strongly, what was only partial, and what remained weak.
- Finally, I’ll end with deployment and the clinical credibility we gained through expert review.

Slide 3 — The Problem
- The problem starts in real clinical workflow.
- A cervical MRI scan gives valuable image data, but the report still depends on time-consuming human review and repeated manual measurements.
- That means the bottleneck is not only image acquisition; it is also the downstream assessement and reporting workflow.

Slide 4 — A patient gets a cervical MRI scan
- From the patient’s perspective, the scan is only the beginning of the process.
- The images still need to be reviewed, the anatomy needs to be assessed, and the important findings need to be extracted into a report.
- So even after the scan is done, the clinically useful answer is not available immediately.

Slide 5 — They Wait
- This waiting happens because radiologists still need to perform repeated review and measurement work.
- Even when the anatomy is visible, turning the scan into a structured and consistent report takes time.
- In busy workflows, that creates backlogs and slows how quickly results get back to the patient and physician.

Slide 6 — And Wait..
- At this point, the issue is not just time; it is also uncertainty.
- The patient is waiting, and the physician is also waiting for a report that is usable for next-step decisions.
- That makes reporting speed and consistency clinically important, not just operationally convenient.

Slide 7 — And Wait Some More..
- Delays in this stage can affect decision-making, follow-up, and treatment planning.
- So the motivation for this project is to reduce repetitive technical workload while keeping the outputs clinically understandable and clinically cautious.

Slide 8 — The Solution
- Our solution is an automated cervical MRI pipeline that helps structure the reporting process.
- Instead of leaving the scan as raw image data, the pipeline organizes it into segmentation outputs, measurements, assessed findings, and a final report.
- The goal is faster access to structured insights while still keeping the radiologist in the loop.

Slide 9 — Faster Insights. Faster Reports
- The pipeline is designed to shorten the path from image to report-ready information.
- It automates measurements that would otherwise need to be repeated manually.
- It also standardizes the way findings are organized, so the output is more consistent from case to case.
- In other words, it supports faster reporting, more consistent measurements, and reduced repetitive workload.

Slide 10 — Pipeline
- The pipeline starts with the MRI scan itself.
- Then we use open-source segmentation engines to isolate the anatomy we care about.
- Once the anatomy is segmented, we extract different groups of measurements.
- Those measurements are then assessed against medical references and organized into structured findings.
- Finally, the reporting layer turns those findings into a physician-facing output.
- We intentionally designed this as a modular system so each stage has a clear responsibility.

Slide 11 — Expected Benefits
- The first expected benefit is reduced reporting time, because repeated measurements no longer have to be done manually every time.
- The second is consistency, because the same extraction logic is applied across cases.
- The third is reduced repetitive workload for radiologists.
- The fourth is smoother workflow, because the outputs are already structured and easier to review.
- Most importantly, this is meant to support radiologists, not replace them.

Slide 12 — Pipeline Architecture
- This section shifts from the clinical motivation into the technical design.
- I’ll now walk through how the pipeline is organized internally.
- The key stages are segmentation, measurement, assessement, and reporting, each with a distinct role.

Slide 13 — 3 segmentation engines, 6 measurement groups, 1 tool
- At a high level, our system uses three segmentation engines, six measurement groups, and one integrated end-to-end workflow.
- We use multiple segmentation engines because different anatomical tasks are handled best by different tools.
- We separate measurements into six groups because not all clinically useful information comes from the same type of anatomy or the same type of computation.
- Even though there are many internal modules, the goal is still one coherent clinical support tool.

Slide 14 — Segmentation Service
- The segmentation stage is built around three specialized engines.
- TotalSpineSeg is the primary broad segmenter, and it gives us vertebrae, discs, and canal-level structure.
- Spinal Cord Toolbox is used for cord-focused analysis, especially when we care about canal and cord measurements or lesion-related screening.
- SPINEPS is used more narrowly for Cobb-related endplate geometry, because precise endplate representation matters for alignment measurements.
- So the main design idea here is: use the right segmentation tool for the right anatomical task instead of forcing one model to do everything.

Slide 15 — Measurement Service: Vertebrae Measurements
- The first measurement branch focuses on vertebral morphometry.
- The main vertebral-body measurement technique is a validated-style morphometry pipeline built directly on the segmentation mask.
- First, the body is isolated in 3D using disc-anchored body isolation so posterior elements do not distort the measurement.
- Then a canal-visible midline band is used to choose the best sagittal slice, and the code can average across the best slice plus or minus one neighbor for stability.
- Vertebral heights are measured with a Genant-style 3-point method: anterior height, middle height, and posterior height.
- AP width is measured with a SHIP-style mid-body AP method, so it is taken from the body itself rather than from a loose bounding box.
- These measurements also generate the vertebral landmarks and corners used later by downstream components such as slippage calculations.
- Conceptually, this is the bone-focused part of the measurement service.

Slide 16 — Measurement Service: Disc Measurements
- The second branch focuses on disc-related structure.
- Here we measure things like disc height, AP ratio, and additional disc appearance features such as signal and bulge-related information.
- The core geometric technique here is per-disc midsagittal slice measurement with PCA-based 4-corner and centerline geometry.
- For each disc, the code finds anterior-superior, anterior-inferior, posterior-superior, and posterior-inferior corners, then computes anterior, middle, and posterior disc heights as well as AP width.
- Disc Height Index is then derived by dividing disc middle height by the mean middle height of the adjacent vertebral bodies.
- Bulge is estimated by comparing disc AP width to adjacent vertebral width and by measuring posterior contour excursion relative to a reference line between adjacent posterior vertebral corners.
- The signal branch then looks inside the disc itself, using raw T2 intensity values to estimate features such as nucleus signal, heterogeneity, and CSF-normalized brightness.
- So this branch combines PCA-based geometry, derived height ratios, posterior contour comparison, and intensity-based signal analysis.
- This branch tries to capture both structural disc geometry and features that may relate to degeneration.
- Later in validation, we found that some disc features were more useful than others, so this branch became a partial rather than a uniformly strong result.

Slide 17 — Measurement Service: Canal / Cord / SAC
- The third branch is the most important one from a validation standpoint.
- It measures the canal, the cord, and the space available for the cord, or SAC.
- The canal measurement uses Spinal Cord Toolbox segmentation followed by slice-by-slice processing.
- More specifically, SCT measures AP diameter on each slice, and the code reports the narrowest stable value by taking the minimum 3-slice rolling median within each vertebral level.
- The cord measurement also uses SCT slice-by-slice processing, but it is aligned to the focal slice chosen by the canal branch so that the two measurements are compared at the same anatomical level.
- SAC is then derived directly as canal AP minus cord AP on the matched slice.
- So the actual technique here is SCT-based per-slice morphometry plus same-slice subtraction for the derived space-available-for-cord metric.
- These are highly relevant for stenosis and compression-related patterns because they quantify how much space remains around the spinal cord.
- This branch ended up being the strongest validated part of the entire pipeline, and it is the main result we emphasize later.

Slide 18 — Measurement Service: Alignment
- The fourth branch focuses on cervical alignment and Cobb-related geometry.
- The actual technique here is endplate-line fitting rather than simple corner-to-corner estimation.
- For the global Cobb angle, the code fits straight lines to the C3 and C7 inferior endplates after canal-cut body isolation, and then measures the angle between those endplate lines.
- The line fitting uses a Theil-Sen approach, which is more robust than a simple least-squares line when boundaries are irregular.
- Segmental angles are computed the same way, but between the inferior endplate of the upper vertebra and the superior endplate of the lower vertebra at each adjacent level.
- So the key alignment technique is robust endplate-line fitting on the segmentation itself.
- Alignment is clinically meaningful, but it is also sensitive to the quality of endplate representation and the imaging context.
- In our validation, this branch was method-valid as a measurement branch, but it was not a strong disease discriminator.

Slide 19 — Measurement Service: Screening Features
- The fifth branch is more screening-oriented than purely geometric.
- It includes fracture-related screening features based on vertebral shape and compression patterns.
- It also includes myelomalacia-related screening features based on cord lesion or signal patterns.
- For fracture-style screening, the code uses a 6-point Genant-style morphometry approach on the vertebral body.
- That means it measures anterior height, middle height, and posterior height, then turns those into deformity patterns such as wedge, biconcave, or crush-style loss.
- In the current runtime integration, the vertebral compression screen is mainly driven by the Ha over Hp ratio, which acts as a cervical deformity screen.
- For myelomalacia-style screening, the pipeline relies on cord-focused lesion or signal analysis rather than plain vertebral geometry.
- So this branch mixes two very different techniques: 6-point vertebral morphometry for fracture-style screening, and cord lesion or signal-based screening for myelomalacia-style review.
- The important point is that these are screening outputs meant to highlight suspicious patterns for review, not to make diagnoses independently.

Slide 20 — Assessement Service
- After measurements are extracted, the assessement stage turns them into readable findings.
- It reviews the measurements, compares them to medical references from the literature, and highlights the patterns that matter most clinically.
- This stage is what transforms isolated numeric outputs into a meaningful review layer.
- It also prepares the findings in a structured way so they can flow directly into the report.
- So this is the point where the system stops being just a measurement pipeline and starts acting like a clinical support pipeline.

Slide 21 — Reporting Service
- The reporting service is the final presentation layer of the pipeline.
- Locally, the reporting package takes the post-assessement contract, normalizes it into a stable report document, and renders human-readable outputs.
- It combines measurements, impressions, patient context, disclaimers, and quality notes into one final physician-facing structure.
- The local reporting code already supports both a clinical-style report and a technical or explainability-style report.
- That means the pipeline is not only producing numbers; it is producing a reportable artifact.

Slide 22 — Validation
- At this point, the important question becomes: does the system actually work in a clinically meaningful way?
- For medical AI, technical structure alone is never enough.
- So the next section focuses on how we validated the measurements and how honestly we assessed those results.

Slide 23 — Validation Approach
- Our validation approach was cohort-based rather than anecdotal.
- We ran the pipeline on healthy and symptomatic cohorts and checked whether different measurements could clearly separate the two populations.
- We also used statistical testing, including p-values, to test whether the separation was meaningful rather than random.
- Low p-values gave stronger evidence that the difference between the groups was real.
- It is also important to say that we did not have unlimited data.
- That means our validation had to be careful and realistic: we focused on the cohorts we actually had, and we avoided pretending that limited data could support stronger claims than it really could.
- In other words, the size of the dataset shaped the strength of our conclusions.
- Most importantly, we did not call everything validated; we only labeled the strongest and most consistent branches as validated.

Slide 24 — Validation Results
- The vertebral compression screen worked well as a screening tool, so that branch was considered validated in screening terms.
- The disc branch gave a partial result: the disc ratio reached an AUC of 0.62, so it showed some discriminatory value, but not enough to claim it as a uniformly strong branch.
- The strongest result came from the canal, cord, and SAC measurements.
- In that branch, canal measurements showed a difference from 11.7 to 8.6 millimeters with p equal to 0.0001.
- SAC changed from 4.7 to 2.3 millimeters, again with p equal to 0.0001.
- Cord measurements changed from 6.3 to 5.5 millimeters with p equal to 0.009.
- So this is the branch where the separation between healthy and symptomatic cohorts was clearest and statistically strongest.
- At the same time, we should be honest that these results come from limited cohorts rather than very large clinical populations.
- So the point of this slide is not to claim that everything is solved, but to show which measurements stayed strong even under that limited-data constraint.

Slide 25 — Validation Results
- Alignment measurements were much weaker as discriminators.
- The alignment branch reached an AUC of 0.57 with p equal to 0.32, which means it did not provide convincing separation between the groups.
- So we present alignment as a useful measurement method, but not as a validated disease discriminator.
- The myelomalacia screen was more encouraging on the specificity side, with about 91 percent healthy specificity.
- The assessement stage is already connected end-to-end, but it has not yet been clinically accuracy-tested as a full downstream decision layer.
- This slide is important because it shows that we kept the strong results strong, and we kept the weak ones honestly labeled as weak.

Slide 26 — Deployment
- After validation, the next question is whether the project exists only as an experiment or whether it can support a deployable workflow.
- We intentionally designed the system as something that can run end-to-end, not just as a notebook demonstration.
- The next slide explains the deployment view.

Slide 27 — Deployment Architecture
- The deployment model is a multi-service cloud architecture.
- In the local repo, that architecture is reflected by separate service areas for the public orchestration entry point, segmentation, measurements, assessement, and reporting.
- The public-facing service is the EEP, or external endpoint, which is the entry point for a case submission.
- Behind it are the internal services, or IEPs, which handle the heavier processing stages such as segmentation and measurement.
- The deployment folders also mirror this design: there is a Docker area for service images, a Compose area for local multi-service orchestration, and a Kubernetes area for cloud deployment manifests.
- Containerization matters because each service has different dependencies, and some of them are heavy, especially the segmentation stage.
- Kubernetes matters because it gives us service separation, orchestration, and scaling, which is important when one part of the system is lightweight and another part depends on GPU-backed deep learning inference.
- The segmentation side is especially important here because we are not running a single model only.
- We use parallel specialized segmentation engines: TotalSpineSeg for broad anatomy, Spinal Cord Toolbox for cord-focused analysis, and SPINEPS for endplate-specific alignment work.
- That means the deployment design needs to support multiple segmentation tools cleanly rather than collapsing them into one fragile environment.
- Monitoring is also part of the architecture. Locally, the measurement service already exposes health and metrics endpoints, and the intended deployment story includes Prometheus and Grafana for observability.
- On the cloud side, AWS is the target platform, and Kubernetes on AWS gives the project a path to a real multi-service deployment rather than a demo-only setup.
- One practical constraint worth mentioning is GPU access.
- The segmentation stage, especially TotalSpineSeg, benefits strongly from GPU-backed inference, and that means deployment planning includes GPU-capable instances and the operational overhead that comes with them.
- So the overall message here is that the project was designed as a deployable system architecture, not only as local research code.
